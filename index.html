5 Patterns of Workplace Mobbing

What to do when you're being systematically targeted at work — based on Leymann's research framework

This tool is for educational purposes only. It is not a clinical assessment, legal advice, or a substitute for professional support. Pattern recognition is a starting point, not a diagnosis. If you are in immediate danger, contact law enforcement. If this situation is affecting your mental or physical health, please seek support from a qualified professional.

Fearless Workplace™ is an educational resource. Nothing here constitutes legal, psychological, or HR consultation.

What is workplace mobbing?

Workplace mobbing is systematic psychological harassment where individuals, groups, or organizations target a single person with hostile actions designed to force them out of their position.

Psychologist Heinz Leymann defined it as hostile communication and unethical conduct directed at one individual, occurring at least once weekly, over at least six months.

One bad day is not mobbing. Systematic targeting over time is.

BullyingOne perpetrator. Often a supervisor abusing positional power. More isolated and direct.
MobbingCollective harassment with organizational participation or complicity. Systematic. The goal is removal.

The distinction matters: mobbing requires organizational-level intervention to stop.

How it typically develops

Leymann identified four phases. Early recognition dramatically changes your options.

1
Critical incident
A conflict or triggering event, often minor
2
Mobbing establishes
Systematic harassment begins, others recruited
3
Management involved
HR/leadership often worsens it by blaming the target
4
Expulsion
Termination, resignation, or medical leave
The 5 patterns
1
Attacks on communication
Your ability to express yourself is systematically restricted
▾
What it looks like
  • Constantly interrupted in meetings
  • Questions ignored or dismissed
  • Your ideas resurface as someone else's
  • Excluded from relevant discussions
  • Emails go unanswered
  • Verbal instructions given, then denied
Why it works

When you can't communicate, you can't defend yourself or contribute. You become invisible or forced into emotional reactions that make you look unstable.

What helps
  • Move to email. Written records are harder to deny or interrupt.
  • Confirm verbal conversations. "Just confirming we agreed on [X]. I'll proceed unless I hear otherwise."
  • Ask task questions only. "What's the deadline?" not "Why did you decide this?"
  • Stay brief and neutral. Don't defend or explain. The more you justify, the more they can twist.
  • Track it privately. Note dates and incidents to see if it's systematic.
The reality: This won't restore your voice. It protects you from being mischaracterized and creates documentation.
2
Attacks on social relations
You're systematically isolated from colleagues and information
▾
What it looks like
  • Left off meeting invitations
  • Removed from email lists
  • Colleagues avoid you
  • Physically isolated
  • Information deliberately withheld
  • Excluded from informal networks
Why it works

Isolation removes your support system. Without witnesses, anything can be said about you. Without information, you can't perform — then you're blamed for poor performance.

What helps
  • Request access in writing. "I wasn't included in [meeting]. Since this affects my work, can you add me?"
  • Connect to work impact. "Without access to X, I can't deliver Y by the deadline."
  • Use shared channels. Post questions publicly to make information gaps visible.
  • Build multiple relationships. Don't rely on one gatekeeper.
  • Show up consistently. Attend everything you're invited to.
The reality: You can't force social inclusion. You can make professional exclusion harder to hide.
3
Attacks on personal reputation
Your character and competence are systematically undermined
▾
What it looks like
  • Vague complaints about your "attitude" or "tone"
  • "Concerned" language: "People are worried about you"
  • Rumors about your competence or stability
  • Normal behavior reframed as problematic
  • Past successes minimized
  • Mistakes magnified and repeatedly mentioned
Why it works

Reputation attacks spread through gossip before you can defend yourself. The more you explain, the more "defensive" you appear.

What helps
  • Stop explaining yourself. Every defense gives them more to twist.
  • Keep communication flat. Brief, task-focused only.
  • Use objective criteria. "This meets the stated requirements."
  • Limit casual conversations. Stick to work topics.
  • Send written summaries. Anchor what actually happened before it gets reframed.
  • Track who repeats what. Patterns reveal who's driving the campaign.
The reality: You can't stop gossip. You can stop feeding it and reduce the material available to twist. When managers drive reputation attacks, individual strategies have severe limits.
4
Attacks on work products
Your work is manipulated to set you up for failure
▾
What it looks like
  • Meaningful work stripped away
  • Impossible workloads with unrealistic deadlines
  • Role scope changed without discussion
  • Contradictory instructions
  • Resources or tools withheld
  • Completed work ignored or criticized without clear criteria
Why it works

Sabotaged work leads to poor performance, which justifies further action against you. The game is rigged.

What helps
  • Confirm scope in writing. "My current priorities are [A, B, C]. Let me know if this changes."
  • Force prioritization. "To add this task, I need to deprioritize something. What's the priority order?"
  • Clarify contradictions. "I'm seeing different direction. Can you confirm which approach?"
  • Keep copies of your work. When contributions get erased, you have proof.
  • Don't volunteer extra. Additional work obscures the sabotage pattern.
  • Document changes. Track task manipulations and conflicting directives with dates.
The reality: These strategies make sabotage visible and create documentation. They don't stop it.
5
Attacks on health
Direct threats to your safety or wellbeing
▾
What it looks like
  • Threats of violence
  • Physical intimidation
  • Sexual harassment
  • Health-risking assignments
  • Exposure to hazardous conditions
  • Public humiliation designed to cause harm
Why this is different

This is no longer about workplace dynamics. This is about safety and liability. Normal strategies don't apply. External intervention is required.

What helps
  • Get yourself out. Remove yourself from danger immediately.
  • Document externally. Email evidence to personal account. Save texts and photos.
  • Preserve everything. Dates, witnesses, medical records, written threats.
  • Get outside help now. Employment attorney. Medical provider. Law enforcement if safety is threatened.
  • Stop all engagement. No conversation, no mediation.
  • Focus on exit. Protect yourself legally and get out safely.
The reality: Organizations rarely protect you at this stage. External support and documentation are essential. Your goal is safe exit, not resolution.
What you need to know
Pattern recognition matters

One bad day is not mobbing. If you're seeing multiple patterns at least weekly over at least six months, that's mobbing, not ordinary conflict.

Early recognition changes everything

Catching it in Phase 1 or 2 gives you significantly more options. Once you hit Phase 3, the situation becomes much harder to resolve internally.

Your strategy shifts over time

Early stage: try to address it directly, clarify, set boundaries. Late stage: document everything, protect yourself legally, plan your exit.

Explaining yourself helps early, hurts later

Initial misunderstandings may benefit from clarification. Once mobbing is systematic, additional explanation becomes ammunition.

Documentation protects you

Writing things down protects you legally and keeps you grounded. In the eyes of the law, if it's not documented, it didn't happen.

Individual strategies have limits

These tactics reduce harm. They don't fix toxic organizations. When you're facing systematic mobbing with organizational complicity, individual response strategies can only do so much. If the organization supports it, your best option is usually strategic exit.
